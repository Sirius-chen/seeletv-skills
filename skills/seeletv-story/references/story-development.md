# Story Development

## Choose the entry point

For an idea, identify the dramatic possibility and develop one useful direction. When the user asks to explore, offer a few alternatives that differ in conflict, point of view, consequence, or outcome; changing names and occupations does not create a different story mechanism.

For an existing synopsis or script, recover its actual premise, relationships, and intended experience before proposing changes. An ambiguous ending or restrained tone can be intentional. Do not turn every project into a twist-driven short drama.

For supplied adaptation material, distinguish retained events from proposed compression, omission, or invention. State coverage when only an excerpt is available. Do not pretend to have read an entire source from a summary.

## Find the dramatic center

Clarify the questions that matter to this story:

- Who wants something specific now, and why does it matter to them?
- What person, condition, obligation, or competing desire makes it difficult?
- What does the character try, what resists, and what changes because of the attempt?
- What choice, discovery, relationship shift, or consequence gives the ending its force?
- What should the audience understand or feel, and what do they know before the characters do?

Use theme as a question explored through behavior and consequence, not a slogan all characters repeat. Internal contradiction can deepen action, but not every character needs a traumatic backstory, mistaken belief, or redemption arc. Preserve non-classical and observational forms when those are the user's intent.

## Build causal beats

A useful short-form progression often has an initial situation, pressure, an attempted tactic, resistance, a consequential turn, and an outcome. Combine, reorder, or omit these labels when the form calls for it. They are planning aids, not a mandatory act count or a timestamp formula.

Connect beats through reasons and consequences. If one event can be swapped with another without changing anything, test whether the sequence needs a clearer cause or a more deliberate associative structure. Escalation can change information, trust, leverage, exposure, resources, or the cost of continuing; it need not mean louder dialogue or greater physical danger.

Let characters make choices with credible alternatives. An opponent can have a defensible goal and need not explain their worldview. Establish the rules or evidence needed for the resolution before asking the audience to accept it. A reveal should change the meaning of known details or a character's next choice, rather than introduce an unrelated solution.

## Select details and scale

Use a few details specific to this person and situation. A prop, interrupted habit, physical task, or awkward spatial relationship can carry emotion and information without explanatory dialogue. Do not manufacture weather, crowds, violence, or backstory merely to add atmosphere.

Respect the intended duration, location count, cast, and production limits. For a very short scene, focus on a complete local exchange or decision rather than compressing an entire feature plot into exposition. Report a material scope conflict and propose a concrete reduction or longer treatment.

## Deliver

Match the request: a logline, concise premise, beat outline, or draft. Planning notes do not substitute for a requested screenplay. Label new choices as proposals; do not present them as facts in the supplied script. Ask for a decision only when competing choices would materially change the requested work.
