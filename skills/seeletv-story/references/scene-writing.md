# Scenes and Dialogue

## Shape the scene

Identify the entering situation, each relevant character's immediate objective and tactic, and the state the scene leaves behind. The change can be informational, relational, emotional, or physical. A scene may also deliberately hold a condition or establish a pattern; do not force a reversal into every scene.

Choose where to enter and leave. Remove setup the audience can infer, while preserving the context required to understand the exchange. End when the scene has delivered its consequence, unresolved pressure, or intended observation. A cliffhanger is optional.

Stage interaction through behavior that belongs to the situation: preparing food, protecting an object, waiting for a reply, making room, or refusing to move. These actions should expose a strategy or relationship, not keep actors arbitrarily busy. Track arrivals, departures, information, and prop transfers so characters do not know or possess things without a cause.

## Write playable action

Use present-tense, observable action in short, readable passages. Describe what can be seen or heard; convey internal experience through chosen behavior, context, or an explicitly requested voice-over. Keep details that affect performance, story, or continuity.

Specify a hand, a marking, or an exact direction when the story depends on it. Do not overload a literary scene with lens settings, camera paths, shot numbers, or frame-by-frame instructions. Those belong in a shot treatment requested separately.

Let reactions follow a stimulus, at a scale that fits the character. A delayed answer, an avoided look, or a changed tactic can be stronger than an explanation of emotion. Stillness is playable when its context and effect are clear.

## Make dialogue act

A line can test, bargain, deflect, reassure, conceal, provoke, or admit. Know what the speaker is trying to get from the listener; the literal subject need not state that objective. Give the response room to change the tactic.

Differentiate voices through priorities, vocabulary, sentence shape, rhythm, and willingness to speak. Avoid caricature, accent spelling as a substitute for characterization, interchangeable witty lines, and biographical exposition both speakers already know.

Use subtext when it serves the scene, without making every line evasive. A direct statement can be the decisive action. Preserve meaningful interruptions, silence, and incomplete thoughts; do not add a pause or an adverb to every line.

Keep character names, dialogue language, and requested format consistent. Distinguish spoken dialogue, voice-over, off-screen speech, and other sounds where that distinction affects production. Do not silently turn a spoken line into narration.

## Check scale and handoff

Estimate duration from the actual dialogue, intended delivery, non-overlapping actions, and necessary reactions. Treat this as an estimate until performed or measured; a fixed words-per-second rule cannot establish exact runtime. If the scene is too dense, propose compression or more time rather than making actors impossibly fast.

Deliver the full requested scene in a readable screenplay form: location/time heading, action, named speakers, and dialogue. Use an existing format when supplied. Preserve uncertainties in accompanying notes instead of inserting administrative labels into the performance text.
