# Directing a Generatable Shot

## Attention and shot purpose

Start from the supplied action, line, observation, or reveal. Decide whose experience organizes the moment, what the viewer must read, what can remain off screen, and what changes by the end. A spatial introduction, held observation, reaction, or detail insert can be a complete purpose; not every shot needs a plot reversal.

Choose scale for the information: a wider view for geography and relationships, a medium view for interaction and hands, a closer view for a decision or readable detail. A distant figure cannot carry a subtle facial performance. A close-up that hides an essential hand action cannot prove that action happened. Compose vertical frames through depth and foreground/background relationships when lateral staging becomes cramped.

## Establish playable geography

Use enough fixed anchors to describe the working space: a door, counter, table, window, path, or other source-established feature. Place visible characters relative to those anchors and each other; specify facing, eye-lines, distance, seated/standing state, and who is entering or off screen when they matter.

Distinguish world position from screen position. A person beside the door remains beside it across a reverse angle, even if they move from screen left to screen right. Read anatomical left/right from the character's perspective; label screen directions separately. Define the working side of an interaction axis in relation to real subjects or anchors, not merely as an unexplained 'left side of the axis.'

For exchanges and hand actions, track the hand, object, contact, transfer path, and resulting ownership. A later shot must not repeat a completed transfer or show an object already received before the transfer occurs. Keep other characters alive through relevant listening or ongoing activity without assigning them competing main actions.

Preserve eye-line and screen travel across cuts. If crossing the axis is intentional, use a visible camera move, a neutral angle, or a new spatial establishment to make the new relationship readable. A deliberate disorienting treatment can differ; do not impose conventional continuity against the user's intent.

## Place and move the camera

Describe the start with shot size, camera height, view direction, and relation to the subjects or a fixed anchor. Use lens language for a useful perspective choice—wide spatial separation, natural conversational perspective, compressed distance—not as a list of premium equipment. Exact focal length is optional and does not guarantee identical framing across models.

A move should direct attention, reveal information, follow action, alter relational pressure, or express an agreed viewpoint. Define:

- The starting frame and relative camera position.
- The event or time that triggers movement.
- Direction/path, subject relationship, and pace.
- The ending frame and when the camera settles.

A pan rotates a camera's view; a track or dolly changes its position; a zoom changes focal length. Say which visible effect is intended. A focus pull shifts attention between established distances without requiring a camera move. An orbit changes view angle and often background geometry; use it only when the space and purpose support it.

For a fixed shot, name the held composition and the action or environment that still moves inside it. Fixed does not mean frozen actors. Several coordinated adjustments can serve one camera intention, but do not simultaneously demand a locked camera and a moving camera without separate phases. A cut begins a new shot, not another camera movement inside the old one.

## Direct performance through change

Ground acting in an immediate objective and response to a stimulus. Translate the important feeling into a few visible choices: gaze shifts, interrupted speech, a hand stopping, breath catching, a jaw releasing, a delayed approach, or a changed distance. Match intensity to the character and shot scale. Do not add every micro-expression to every second.

Describe the progression rather than conflicting simultaneous emotions. For example, a confident explanation falters after the listener shows an object; the speaker's eyes settle on it before they try to recover their smile. Preserve which character knows what, and when the viewer may see an identity or clue.

Blocking carries intention: making room can invite, occupying a threshold can prevent departure, turning away can deny an answer. Give movements a purpose and achievable path. Do not add unmotivated walking, tears, threats, or physical contact merely to make the frame active.

## Light, environment, and sound

Carry the supplied visual direction into concrete source light, contrast, color relationships, and relevant material behavior. Preserve light direction and intrinsic costume/prop colors across coverage. Do not invent rain, smoke, crowds, damage, or moving light to decorate a scene.

Describe only meaningful environmental motion and sound. Assign dialogue to its exact speaker; distinguish on-camera speech, off-screen speech, narration, effects, ambience, and music. Preserve required words and the specified language. A reaction or cut can occur during continuing speech, but the line must not restart in the next shot. Basic sound timing belongs in the video prompt; a new score or voice-production workflow does not.
